The Abstraction Penalty: Why the Best PMs Stay Close to the Work

Product Thinking • September 24, 2026

The Distance Problem

Every PM knows they should talk to users. But most don't — not because they don't care, but because the organization gives them so many ways to avoid it.

There's the analytics dashboard. The research team's quarterly report. The customer success Slack channel. The NPS score. The support ticket taxonomy. Each of these is useful. Together, they create a comfortable illusion that you understand your users without ever talking to one.

What Gets Lost in Translation

Data tells you what happened. Research reports tell you what someone heard. Slack threads tell you what one person complained about. None of these tell you what it's actually like to use your product — the friction, the workarounds, the quiet frustrations that never make it into a ticket.

The abstraction penalty isn't just about missing information. It's about missing the right kind of information. Quantitative data shows you patterns but not reasons. Qualitative research shows you stated preferences but not actual behavior. Only direct observation — watching someone use your product in their natural environment — reveals the gap between what people say and what they do.

The Three Layers of Separation

Layer 1: Data instead of observation. Dashboards are clean, precise, and misleading. They show you what users clicked, not why they hesitated. They measure what you thought to instrument, not what actually matters. When your primary interface with users is a chart, you're managing abstractions, not people.

Layer 2: Reports instead of conversations. User research is invaluable, but a research report is not a user interview. By the time an interview becomes a finding, then a slide, then a recommendation, the texture is gone. The frustration in someone's voice, the moment they gave up, the workaround they invented — these don't survive the journey to a summary document.

Layer 3: Stakeholders instead of users. In larger organizations, PMs increasingly hear about users through other people. The support team's priorities. The sales team's feature requests. The CEO's latest customer dinner story. Each retelling adds a layer of interpretation. You're not hearing the user anymore — you're hearing someone else's theory about the user.

Staying Close to the Work

The best PMs resist abstraction through deliberate practices:

Weekly user contact. Not quarterly. Weekly. Even 15 minutes of watching someone use your product resets your intuition. You'll notice things no dashboard showed you — the awkward pause, the confused double-tap, the sigh before they figure it out.

Read support tickets yourself. Don't just look at the categorization. Read the raw text. The actual words users choose reveal how they think about your product — and what mental model you're failing to match. Users don't report bugs; they report their experience of something being broken.

Watch sessions, don't just count them. Session recordings and screen replays show you what analytics can't: where people pause, where they rage-click, where they abandon a flow you thought was working because your funnel report said the dropoff was "acceptable."

Talk to users before reading the research. Form your own hypotheses first. If you read the report before the interview, you'll find exactly what the researcher found — and miss what they didn't think to look for.

The Cost of Distance

Teams that operate at a distance from users don't just make worse decisions — they make confidently wrong decisions. Data gives you false certainty. Reports give you false understanding. Stakeholders give you false confidence. You ship features that address problems users never had, while ignoring the friction they live with every day.

The antidote isn't abandoning data or research. It's recognizing that these are supplements to user contact, not replacements for it. Every layer of abstraction costs something — a nuance, a context, a truth. The question isn't whether you can afford to stay close to users. It's whether you can afford not to.
