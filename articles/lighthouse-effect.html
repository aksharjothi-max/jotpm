The Lighthouse Effect: Why Product Teams Get Stuck Maintaining Instead of Building

AJ
Akshar Jothi
Product Manager | Growth Strategy

Lighthouses don't chase ships. They stand in one place, rotating their beam, making sure every vessel knows where the rocks are. They're essential — but they don't go anywhere.

Many product teams are lighthouses. They stand still, maintaining the same features, fixing the same bugs, rotating through the same backlog. They're essential to the business — but they're not going anywhere either.

I call this the Lighthouse Effect: when a team becomes so consumed by maintaining existing value that they stop creating new value.

The Maintenance Trap

It happens gradually. A team starts building features. Those features need bug fixes. Those bug fixes reveal technical debt. The technical debt requires refactoring. The refactoring introduces new bugs. Suddenly, 80% of the team's capacity is spent keeping the lights on.

This isn't lazy engineering — it's a natural consequence of success. The more you build, the more there is to maintain. And if you don't deliberately fight this gravity, maintenance will consume your entire roadmap.

The worst part? Nobody notices. On paper, the team looks productive. Tickets are closed. Bugs are fixed. Systems are stable. But the product is standing still while the market moves forward.

How the Lighthouse Effect Sneaks Up on You

1. The "Quick Fix" Spiral

Every quick fix seems reasonable. But quick fixes accumulate into architecture decisions. Over time, the system becomes a patchwork of workarounds that's harder and harder to change.

2. The Support Queue Black Hole

Customer support tickets always feel urgent. And they are — for the customer. But if your entire backlog is support requests, you're not building product — you're just holding it together.

3. The "Tech Debt" Excuse

Tech debt is real, but it's also become the go-to explanation for why teams can't deliver new features. Sometimes it's legitimate. Sometimes it's a symptom of deeper issues — lack of ownership, fear of change, or a team that's lost its appetite for building.

4. The Metrics Mirage

Your dashboards look great — uptime 99.9%, latency low, error rates minimal. But these are maintenance metrics. They tell you nothing about whether you're building the right things for the future.

"A team that only maintains what it has will slowly lose to a team that builds what's next. The lighthouse guides ships — but it never reaches the shore."

Breaking Free: From Lighthouse to Ship

How do you escape the Lighthouse Effect? You need to deliberately shift from maintenance mode to building mode. Here's the playbook:

1. Ring-Fence Maintenance Capacity

Allocate a fixed percentage of capacity to maintenance — say 30%. Everything else goes to new building. This forces hard conversations about what maintenance is truly essential and what can be deferred.

Yes, this means some bugs don't get fixed immediately. Some tech debt lingers. Some support tickets take longer. That's the cost of progress.

2. Kill Features, Not Just Bugs

Most teams are good at shipping features and bad at killing them. But every feature you ship is a feature you maintain. Regularly audit your product and remove features that aren't pulling their weight.

This feels counterintuitive — you spent months building something, and now you're deleting it? Yes. Because maintenance is a tax on complexity. Killing features reduces complexity and frees capacity.

3. Measure Building, Not Just Maintaining

Track what percentage of your team's capacity goes to new value creation vs. maintenance. If it's below 50%, you have a problem. Make this metric visible — it should be as prominent as your uptime dashboard.

4. Create a "Builder" Role

Consider splitting your team into builders and operators. Builders focus on new features, new products, new growth. Operators maintain the existing system, handle support, and manage incidents.

This doesn't have to be a hard wall — people can rotate. But it ensures that maintenance doesn't consume everyone's attention equally.

The Courage to Build

Breaking the Lighthouse Effect requires courage. It means accepting short-term maintenance pain for long-term product progress. It means telling stakeholders that some bugs won't be fixed because you're building something more important.

But the alternative is worse. A team that maintains forever eventually maintains a product that nobody wants anymore. The market moves on. Competitors build what's next. And the lighthouse, however well-maintained, becomes irrelevant.

Conclusion

Your product is a ship, not a lighthouse. It needs to move — toward new markets, new users, new value. Maintenance keeps you afloat, but it's not the destination.

Fight the Lighthouse Effect. Ring-fence maintenance. Kill features. Measure building. Create builder roles. And most importantly, have the courage to stop maintaining and start building.

Because in the time it takes to polish the lens, someone else has already reached the shore.
