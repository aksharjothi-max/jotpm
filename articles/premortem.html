The Pre-Mortem: How to Kill Your Product Before It Launches

AJ
Akshar Jothi
Student · Writer · Builder

Most teams run post-mortems — autopsies after something has already died. They gather in a conference room, dissect what went wrong, and document lessons that everyone nods at and nobody applies. The pre-mortem flips this: you imagine the failure before it happens, and you use that imagination to prevent it.

The concept comes from psychologist Gary Klein. Instead of asking "what might go wrong?" — which invites vague, hypothetical worry — you tell a specific story: "It's six months from now. Our launch was a disaster. Write down exactly why."

This shift from abstract to concrete changes everything. When you ask a team to predict problems hypothetically, optimism bias kicks in. People don't want to seem negative. They hedge. They say "we might face some adoption challenges." But when you tell them the failure has already happened, you give them permission to be specific. They'll say "users couldn't find the export button" or "the onboarding flow had seven steps and nobody finished it."

Why PMs Skip This Step

Pre-mortems feel uncomfortable. You're essentially asking a team that's excited about a launch to spend an hour imagining failure. It can seem like you're lacking confidence or being pessimistic. But the best PMs understand that pre-mortem pain is cheaper than post-mortem regret.

Three forces prevent teams from doing pre-mortems:

  • The momentum trap. When you're close to launch, every conversation feels like it should be about shipping, not questioning. Slowing down to imagine failure feels like a luxury you can't afford.
  • The consensus illusion. If everyone agrees the plan is solid, a pre-mortem seems unnecessary. But consensus is exactly when you need it most — groupthink kills more products than bad strategy.
  • The expertise paradox. Senior teams skip pre-mortems because they've "done this before." Experience makes you faster at execution but worse at imagining failure. You've learned to ship, not to doubt.

Running a Pre-Mortem That Actually Works

A pre-mortem isn't a brainstorming session. It's a structured exercise with specific rules:

1. Set the Scene with Precision

Don't say "imagine it went badly." Say: "It's March 17, 2027. We launched the new dashboard six weeks ago. Adoption is 40% below target. Churn has spiked. The CEO just asked for a full review. What happened?" The more specific the scenario, the more specific the answers.

2. Write Individually, Then Share

Give everyone five minutes to write their failure story alone. This prevents anchoring — if the first person says "performance issues," everyone else will echo it. Independent writing surfaces diverse risks. Then go around the room and collect every reason. No filtering, no debating. Just collect.

3. Cluster and Prioritize

Group the reasons into themes. You'll usually find that 80% of failures cluster into 3-4 categories: technical risk, user adoption, market timing, operational readiness. Now you can prioritize. Which risks are most likely? Which would be most damaging? Focus your pre-launch energy there.

4. Assign Owners and Deadlines

Every identified risk gets an owner and a mitigation deadline. Not a vague "we'll keep an eye on it" — a specific action with a date. "By Friday, Sarah will test the onboarding flow with five users who've never seen the product." If you can't assign an owner, the risk is already winning.

"The teams that run pre-mortems don't predict the future — they just stop being surprised by it."

The Hidden Benefit

Beyond risk identification, pre-mortems do something subtle but powerful: they create shared ownership of failure. When a team collectively imagines what could go wrong, they stop blaming individuals when things go sideways. The failure was already discussed. It was already on the list. Now it's just a known risk that materialized, not a personal mistake.

This psychological safety is the real product. Teams that can discuss failure openly before it happens are teams that recover faster when it does. They don't waste energy on blame. They move straight to solutions.

When to Run One

You don't need a pre-mortem for every feature. Save them for launches that matter: new products, major redesigns, market expansions, pricing changes. Any time the cost of failure is high enough that you'd lose sleep over it, spend 60 minutes imagining that sleepless night before it arrives.

The best time to run a pre-mortem is two weeks before launch — close enough that the plan is concrete, far enough that you can still change it. Run it too early and the plan is too vague. Run it too late and you've already committed.

The Paradox of Preparation

Teams that run pre-mortems launch with more confidence, not less. They've already faced the worst-case scenario in a conference room. They've already made their peace with it. When they ship, they ship with eyes open — not because they're pessimistic, but because they've done the work to see clearly.

Your product will fail at something. The question is whether you discover that in a retrospective or a pre-mortem. One costs you users. The other costs you an hour.
